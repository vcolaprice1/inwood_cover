INTERPRETING THE RECONSTRUCTIONS

Methods & sources

Map chronology

The nominal reference dates are around 1875 and 1914. Each reconstruction combines the available sheets, whose dates are listed below. When a target-year sheet was unavailable, the project used the closest available map. Substitute sheets remain explicitly dated; they are not treated as observations of the target year.

Colour maps: Third Military Survey

Forest extraction follows the project’s QGIS workflow for georeferenced colour maps:

  1. Crop and sample. Crop the raster to the study extent while preserving its native resolution. Draw at least 30 small, spatially distributed training polygons per class, avoiding edges, lettering and roads; distinguish forest, non-forest and, where useful, water and confusing map symbols.
  2. Classify and inspect. Use the dzetsaka Random Forest classifier, inspect locations outside the training samples, and add samples to correct recurrent errors. Extract a binary forest mask from the appropriate class value.
  3. Clean and polygonise. Apply a Sieve filter (starting threshold: 10–20 pixels; 8-connected neighbourhood), polygonise the mask with 8-connectivity where available, and retain the forest class.
  4. Refine the polygons. Reproject to an appropriate metric CRS, repair geometries, split multipart features, and retain forest patches of at least 5,000 m² (0.5 ha). The guide specifies filling holes below 1 km², followed by visual checking and manual restoration of genuine clearings, water, settlements and other non-forest openings.
  5. Generalise and validate. Apply Douglas–Peucker simplification (10 m), one smoothing iteration (offset 0.25, maximum angle 180°), repair geometries, dissolve, and split multipart features. Compare the raw and cleaned layers against the map, checking omissions, false positives, narrow patches, restored openings and area changes.

These are the guide’s documented settings, initially developed for Comelico; suitability must be checked for each map. They describe preparation of the supplied research vectors and are not reapplied by this atlas. The reconstructed forest follows historical map symbols, legends and preservation conditions.

Black-and-white maps: dependent vectorisation

The black-and-white maps were vectorised using dependent vectorisation: an existing forest layer is edited against the other map rather than tracing every date independently. The approach follows Bednarczyk, Kaim & Ostafin (2016), retaining common boundaries where the evidence indicates no change and modifying them where a change is supported by the map.

Sae-Jung, Chen & Phuong (2008, especially p. 827) provides the complementary framework for interpreting positional errors and sliver polygons generated by overlay. Its error simulations and split-and-merge correction are not implemented in this atlas.

Backdating and available map dates

The reconstruction uses the backdating and updating principles discussed by Kaim et al. (2016) and Feranec et al. (2007): a reference vector layer is revised using evidence from maps of another period. Backdating describes this editing direction. Using the nearest available sheet is the project’s separate source-selection rule, documented in the chronology above. It does not remove temporal uncertainty or justify interpolating annual rates.

Area calculation and change detection

Areas are calculated after geometry repair and dissolution in ETRS89 / LAEA Europe (EPSG:3035). Overlaps within a reconstruction are counted once. 1 km² = 100 ha. Persistent forest is the intersection of both reconstructions; gain is later minus earlier; loss is earlier minus later. Net change is gain minus loss, and percentage change uses the earlier forest area as its denominator.

All supplied forest coverage is retained, without clipping to administrative or imperial boundaries. Austria-Hungary and its historical Lands are shown as outlines from the supplied 1910 boundary dataset. The Lands reproduce its source groupings, rather than asserting a uniform administrative status. Belluno includes the supplied provincial boundary and Cortina d’Ampezzo, Livinallongo del Col di Lana and Colle Santa Lucia; their separate municipal outlines refer to 1991.

Map display uses a 5 m simplification and hides components smaller than 25 m². Statistics and full GeoJSON downloads retain all analytical components without those display filters. These web display settings are distinct from the source-extraction thresholds above.

Forest share of study area = forest area within the study boundary ÷ total study area × 100. The denominator is the full study-area boundary, including non-forest land and water. For Belluno it includes the three present-day municipalities. Forest outside that boundary remains visible and is included in the full-coverage totals, but is excluded from this percentage. The difference between the two forest shares is expressed in percentage points, distinct from relative net forest-area change. All areas use the unsimplified EPSG:3035 analysis.

Interpretation and limitations

Apparent differences may also reflect scale, georeferencing, map conventions, classification and digitisation. Small change polygons are not automatically evidence of real forest gain or loss. Comparable survey coverage is assumed because no independent survey-footprint mask was supplied. Sheet substitutions make some local comparison intervals unequal or overlapping, particularly in Belluno. Porohy’s date remains uncertain. The atlas does not estimate annual change rates.

Basemaps and software

The natural-features basemap uses present-day OpenStreetMap data via OpenFreeMap and OpenMapTiles, styled without labels, transport, buildings or settlements. Water features may include artificial lakes. Its pale land-cover shading is modern context, not part of the historical reconstruction. Standard OSM is also available. Natural Earth is used only as an explicitly announced fallback if vector rendering fails. Mapping: Leaflet and MapLibre GL JS.
